Definitions, rules, and first principles are rarely the most difficult elements of a story to communicate. Tradition merely insists they be concealed.

Most authors therefore elect to introduce them indirectly. They construct characters, furnish them with ambitions, fears, habits, and carefully selected imperfections, then allow the architecture of their universe to emerge as an accidental by-product of conflict. Ideally, the reader arrives at the final page convinced he has experienced a story, never realizing he has merely been instructed:

> *A young man has just been admitted to Harvard Law.*
>
> *Across a crowded bistro sits a woman with whom he has already imagined several decades of shared disappointment and improbable happiness. She has not yet noticed him.*
>
> *His friends insist that opportunities of this sort, once ignored, persist indefinitely as regret.*
>
> *His own experience suggests otherwise. Rejection, he estimates, rarely exceeds one minute.*
>
> *He rises.*
>
> *On the walk between their tables, he rehearses three opening sentences.*
>
> *The first sounds rehearsed.*
>
> *The second belongs to someone considerably more handsome.*
>
> *The third is acceptable.*
>
> *She looks up.*
>
> *He smiles.*
>
> *He says—*
>
> *The floor convulses.*
>
> *Every wine glass shatters after the first wave.*
>
> *The lights extinguish.*
>
> *A bottle strikes the stone floor between them. Glass fragments scatter across his shins and into her leg.*
>
> *Neither learns the other’s name.*
>
> *It was the fourth earthquake that week.*

## Chapter 42

It was a cruel day of April.

The dusk had come, and the lilies bloomed in the vase on the café table in front of Miguel.

He blankly stared at the menu, pretending that he was trying to choose something to drink.

He didn’t want to drink. He didn’t want to eat.

His stomach ached so badly he might as well go to the bathroom before she arrived and puke.

He stood up.

She had just entered.

“Oh, you got in early?”

Miguel nodded.

“Sorry I was late. Tough day at work. Michael insisted I stay for an extra hour to finish something.”

“Yeah, I got your reasoning. You sent it before, right?”

“Yeah. That was me.”

“How is Michael?”

“What was so important? Not that I wasn’t willing to wait for you. Just curious.”

“It’s something I cannot tell you, hon. Company confidentiality agreement.”

“You do know I’m the director of the project between our companies, right?”

“Yes. But that in particular is an embarrassing kind of confidentiality detail. Sorry. I really cannot say.”

“Well…” Miguel smiled. “I thought maybe we’d order that cocktail you love so much.”

“Rum daiquiri?”

“Yes. But I asked the bartender for some extra changes. See if you could notice.”

“Oh, God. Okay. Challenge accepted. Also now it’s my turn to dare you.”

Melana signaled to the waiter.

He took a beat to arrive.

“Yes, madam. What will you have?”

“Oh no. This one is for him.”

The waiter looked at Miguel.

“What will it be?”

“He’ll take a Vieux Carré. Rye and Cognac, equal parts. Benedictine. Peychaud’s and Angostura. Orange peel, expressed.”

The waiter nodded.

“And chilled.”

“Of course.”

“And for her, rum daiquiri. Remember?”

“With a little less sugar,” Melana added.

The waiter smiled warmly at her.

“Yes. I remember.”

He left.

Miguel looked at her.

“You ordered for me.”

“I know what you like.”

“You’re assuming.”

“I’m usually right.”

“We’ll wait and see,” he said.

A few minutes later, the waiter returned.

Both drinks were placed before them in perfect geometry.

Miguel’s was dark amber, almost copper beneath the café lights, with a narrow twist of orange peel resting against the rim.

Melana’s was pale and cold, with a thin layer of foam and lemon zest staining the rim.

Miguel took a sip.

He waited.

“Well?”

“Annoyingly good.”

“Yeah. I know.”

She tasted his.

“Too complicated.”

“You ordered it.”

“Exactly.”

They laughed.

Miguel took another sip.

“I met Michael today. At HQ in San Francisco.”

“Oh, really? When?”

“In the morning.”

“How was the trip back?”

“It was fine. I need to fix my watch again because of that.”

“The price of relativity. You really should get a new one. There are proper solutions, you know.”

“Mm. No. It’s my father’s.”

Miguel looked at the watch.

“You’re still wearing it?”

“Of course.”

“All these years?”

She nodded.

“And you said you couldn’t take it off.”

“I can’t.”

“Why?”

She looked down at it.

“I made a Vawlt.”

Miguel paused.

“I see.”

“I needed to keep something of him. It’s the only item that survived.”

“I didn’t know.”

“No need to apologize.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You were.”

He smiled.

“Nothing escapes your gaze.”

She took another sip.

“Oof.”

“What?”

“Nothing.”

She looked at him.

“You’re thinking.”

“No more than everyone else.”

“Dangerous.”

A silence passed between them.

Then Melana looked up.

“It’s all deterministic anyway, no?”

Miguel leaned back.

“Not exactly.”

“But in general?”

“In general, yes.”

She looked at her drink.

“Then what’s the difference?”

“Between what?”

“Between deciding something and it happening.”

Miguel considered the question.

“Intent.”

She smiled.

“That sounds like an answer designed to avoid the question.”

“It probably is.”

“Good.”

She took his glass from him, switched it with hers, and took another sip.

“Mine’s better.”

“Which mine?”

“The one I got.”

“That’s cheating.”

“You ordered mine.”

“Exactly.”

She smiled.

“I’m delighted to announce I’m officially glad you asked me for a second date.”

Miguel looked at her.

“Yeah?”

“This is quite new for me.”

“Nice to hear.”

He took another sip.

Now he was beginning to feel tipsy.

He looked at the glass.

Then at her.

“Yours is perfectly balanced.”

“I know.”

“But it’s missing something.”

“Oh?”

Miguel looked at her.

He paused.

Melana watched him.

A few seconds passed.

She laughed.

“What are you doing?”

“Thinking.”

“That long?”

“I’m being thorough.”

“You’re ridiculous.”

“I’ve been told.”

“Frequently?”

“Only by people who don’t understand me.”

“That sounds convenient.”

“It is.”

She smiled.

For a moment, neither of them spoke.

Miguel reached for her hand.

His fingers touched the watch.

“You know,” he said, “I think mine’s missing something too.”

“What?”

“A taste I can’t quite place.”

She looked at him.

He leaned across the table and kissed her.

The waiter looked over from the far end of the bar.

Melana smiled.

“You’re getting brave.”

“I’m learning.”

“From whom?”

“Several sources.”

She shook her head.

“You’re impossible.”

They stayed for another hour.

They talked about the project.

About San Francisco.

About Michael.

“He drives me crazy,” Melana said. “He always wants something. And he never stops arguing.”

Miguel said nothing.

She noticed.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You don’t like him.”

“I barely know him.”

“You met him today.”

“Exactly.”

She smiled.

They moved on.

They gossiped about a professor they both disliked.

They argued about whether a person could be brilliant and still be wrong.

Eventually, Melana looked at the broken time on her wrist.

“We should go.”

Miguel nodded.

The evening had become cold.

They walked without deciding where they were going.

After a while, Melana said:

“Michael thinks the new architecture will take another six months.”

“Four.”

She stopped.

“Four?”

Miguel looked at her.

“Six is too conservative.”

“How do you know?”

He hesitated.

Then smiled.

“Because he’s wrong.”

She studied him.

“You’re impossible.”

They continued walking.

She had deliberately withheld the timeline.

Miguel had supplied it anyway.

Melana was slightly disturbed.

The kiss had been unsettlingly good.

Miguel enjoyed each moment.

Later, he thought, *I was pretty good, wasn’t I?*

*Very well done indeed.*

He smiled to himself.
