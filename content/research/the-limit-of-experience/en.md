## TL;DR

The thesis is simple: consciousness lets us know that we exist, understand that we are finite, and think about death, infinity, God, and other minds — but it does not let us fully experience or contain them. We can approach truth and build models of it without the model becoming identical with the thing itself.

At death, on this hypothesis, the organism really dies and the world continues; yet consciousness cannot experience the state in which it is no longer present. Death is therefore an objective ending but a subjective boundary: the world can finish a person, while that person cannot experience themselves as already finished.

## Abstract

This paper proposes a model in which the Tree of Knowledge represents the emergence of recursive self-consciousness — the capacity of a subject to represent itself across time and to conceive its own absence — while the Tree of Life represents the missing capacity to contain the full implications of that knowledge. The result is an asymmetry: a subject can represent a boundary it cannot experience from beyond. The same form recurs in death, other minds, prophecy, and divine knowledge. Zeno's dichotomy is used not as a refutation of motion but as a formal image of an experiential boundary. The central claim is that death may be objectively complete while its completion cannot itself become first-person experience.

## 1. From knowledge to recursive consciousness

A naïve reading of the Tree of Knowledge as the creation of intelligence is incompatible with Maimonides: Adam understands a command before the transgression and therefore already possesses intellect (*Guide of the Perplexed* I.2). What is needed is a distinction between first-order cognition — perception, memory, desire, inference — and recursive consciousness: the capacity to represent oneself as an object extended through time. Once a subject can formulate "I exist," it can also formulate "I may cease to exist." Death thereby becomes not only a biological event but a conceptual problem.

On the proposed model, Eve is the first to cross this threshold and Adam follows; their descendants inherit a form of consciousness, not inherited guilt. This respects Ezekiel 18:20: "the son shall not bear the iniquity of the father." The Tree of Life is then construed as the metaphysical complement of Knowledge: not mere duration, but the missing capacity of recursive consciousness to contain the infinity it can already conceive. Rashi and Nahmanides on Genesis 3:22 connect the Tree of Life with overcoming mortality or approaching a godlike condition; the cognitive completion proposed here is an original development of that textual structure, not a claim that they themselves held this view.

## 2. Life, sin, and justice

The model cannot identify the desire to live with sin, because Deuteronomy explicitly commands "choose life" (Deut. 30:19). The relevant distinction is between choosing finite life and demanding that life be immune to finitude. Medicine, procreation, memory, art, and love are not sins; the problem begins when a finite good becomes a metaphysical entitlement: not merely "I choose life," but "life must not be allowed to end."

Punishment can therefore be reformulated. God need not be imagined as inventing an additional torture after death. In the spirit of Maimonidean restraint concerning divine attributes, "punishment" can name the relation between a perfect order and the structure of a finite subject. The ancestral act transmits vulnerability, not moral debt, and each person reenacts the problem through their own relation to finitude. Job further limits theodicy: visible suffering is not a receipt proving guilt. No particular historical catastrophe therefore licenses a uniquely certain interpretation of divine judgment; post-Holocaust Jewish theology, including Hans Jonas and Emil Fackenheim, reinforces this caution.

## 3. Private worlds and other minds

No subject directly possesses another subject's experience. We observe bodies, speech, and behavior; we never enter the other's first-person point of view. Leibniz offers a useful formal precedent: monads are "windowless" centers of representation coordinated within one order without the contents of one mind passing into another (*Monadology* §7). The present model radicalizes this possibility: each recursively conscious subject inhabits a private phenomenal world, while other genuine subjects exist as centers of phenomenal worlds of their own; their appearance in my world is a synchronized representation rather than their first-person experience itself.

The technical term *Caritus* may designate this metaphysical class. If membership is inherited through ordinary genealogy from Adam and Eve, it cannot remain identical over time with a fixed religious or ethnic identity: models of human genealogy show that sufficiently ancient ancestors spread rapidly through the pedigrees of connected populations (Rohde, Olson & Chang, 2004). Biblical "chosenness" is therefore more coherently interpreted as a vocation of revelation and responsibility rather than a permanent biological monopoly on consciousness.

The inaccessibility of another mind does not turn love into proof. Buber's I–Thou relation instead suggests an ethical response: another person is conceptually diminished when reduced to an object that can be completely possessed by knowledge. Relation therefore requires trust in an otherness that remains epistemically irreducible.

## 4. Freedom, prophecy, and representation

Perfect divine knowledge appears to threaten freedom, yet Maimonides maintains both genuine human choice and complete divine knowledge while warning against treating God's knowledge as enlarged human prediction (*Mishneh Torah*, Hilkhot Teshuvah 5). A complete description of an action need not be its cause. A total order may therefore include free choices without converting them into coercion.

Human knowledge is likewise representational. An intuition, symbol, number, or myth may be a low-dimensional projection of a richer structure. Error begins when representation is identified with the represented. Prophecy, following Maimonides' account in *Guide* II.36–45, can therefore be understood as apprehension of architecture rather than exhaustive data: the prophet grasps relations and rules and renders them as image, law, or parable without becoming omniscient. Genuine structural insight accordingly grants no license to assimilate every historical event as confirmation of a single theory.

## 5. Zeno and the moment of death

Zeno's dichotomy does not show that physical motion never reaches its destination. Aristotle already distinguished a continuum from its potentially infinite divisions, and modern analysis rigorously describes convergent sums. Its importance here is different: it supplies a formal image of the difference between approach and completion from the standpoint of experience.

Epicurus argued that when we exist death is absent, and when death is present we are absent (*Letter to Menoeceus* 124–125). The present hypothesis retains the structure but reverses the therapeutic conclusion: if the subject's nonexistence cannot itself be a content of that subject's experience, then death is a real boundary of experience without being another experiential state.

The central postulate is compressed as:

<p class="formula">L —[ΔT → 0]→ D</p>

Here *L* denotes the sequence of lived experiences, *ΔT* the objective temporal interval between successive experiential states, and *D* the boundary of death. The equation is not an experimentally established neurological law; it is a formal shorthand for the metaphysical hypothesis. As the stream approaches its terminal boundary, the intervals between experiential states tend toward zero, while *D* never becomes an experiential member of the sequence. From the outside, the organism may die in finite time; from the inside, there can be no experience whose content is "I now do not exist." Death may therefore be objectively complete while remaining unavailable as a completed first-person experience.

## Conclusion

Death, infinity, other minds, prophecy, and divine knowledge share a common structure: the limitation of standpoint. Recursive consciousness can represent realities that cannot become complete objects within its own first-person perspective. The fundamental human error is therefore not ignorance but the inference that, because truth exists, a finite subject must be capable of possessing it exhaustively.

The model proposes the opposite possibility. Reality may be more exact than consciousness can contain. Another person may be real without my inhabiting their interiority; a choice may be free even if perfectly known from a divine standpoint; and death may be an objective ending without becoming the experience "the ending has already occurred." At death, on the theory, the organism ends; the stream of experience converges toward *D*; yet *D*, defined by the absence of the experiencer, cannot occur as a member of that stream. The world can finish a person — the person cannot experience themselves as already finished.

Humanity, on this account, acquired enough knowledge to discover the boundary, but not enough being to stand outside it.
